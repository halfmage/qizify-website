A mechatronics apprentice learns several trades at once: mechanical, electrical, fluid power, controls. Most of that learning happens on the shop floor. In the US, the federal rule recommends 144 hours of classroom instruction a year: less than four working weeks. So many theory questions come up at work, where a trainer is not always free to answer them.

This article shows what year one actually demands, how little instruction time apprentices get in Germany, the US and England, and what the research says about AI tutors filling the gap. It ends with three questions to test any AI tool before your apprentices use it.

## How much classroom time mechatronics apprentices get

Mechatronics apprenticeships look different in each country, but they share one feature: the job is the main classroom. The table puts the three systems side by side.

The definitions differ, so read the numbers as orders of magnitude, not as a league table. The point holds anyway: in every system, an apprentice spends most of their time at work, and most theory questions come up there.

## What year one of a mechatronics apprenticeship demands

Germany's curriculum is public and spells out, year by year, what a mechatronics apprentice has to learn, which makes it a useful reference wherever you train. The [KMK framework curriculum](https://www.kmk.org/fileadmin/Dateien/pdf/Bildung/BeruflicheBildung/rlp/Mechatroniker98-01-30-Stand-18-02-23.pdf) packs five learning fields and 320 lesson hours into year one, more than any later year.

    ["3: Installing electrical equipment safely (100)", "Electrical quantities and calculations, DC and AC circuits, protective measures, testing to standard", "Can they calculate current and power on a real circuit, then measure it?"],
    ["4: Energy and information flows (60)", "Basic control circuits, pneumatic and hydraulic quantities and calculations, circuit diagrams", "Can they read a pneumatic diagram and predict what happens when a valve switches?"],
    ["5: Working with IT systems (40)", "Operating systems, networks, data protection and security, access rights, documentation", "Do they know which systems they may access at work, and why?"]

Two details are easy to miss. The curriculum teaches maths and technical English inside the learning fields, not as separate ones, and it builds on the lowest German school-leaving certificate. And English appears in all five fields, from vocabulary to product manuals. An apprentice who struggles with either struggles a little everywhere.

Early gaps also cost later. German mechatronics apprentices sit Part 1 of their final exam before the end of year two: eight hours built around a practical task on a mechatronic subsystem. It counts for 40% of the final grade ([§ 8 MechatronikerAusbV](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/mechatronikerausbv/BJNR151600011.html)). Whatever was only half understood in year one shows up in the certificate.

## What the research says about AI tutors

In a study of IT apprentices, researchers at Germany's Federal Institute for Vocational Education and Training (BIBB) found generative AI already firmly part of their working day. The question is what it does to learning. There is no randomised study of AI tutors in apprenticeship training yet, as far as we could find. There are four studies close enough to learn from.

<StackedTable
  headers={["Study", "Setting", "What happened", "Lesson for apprentice training"]}
  rows={[
    ["BIBB experiment, Germany (Datenreport 2026, C3.3)", "27 second-year IT apprentices, with and without ChatGPT", "The ChatGPT group solved more tasks, faster, with less frustration, but understood code no better and on some tasks worse.     ["Bastani et al., PNAS 2025", "Nearly 1,000 high school maths students in Turkey, randomised by class", "Plain ChatGPT: 48% better in practice, 17% worse on the later exam without AI.     ["Kestin et al., Scientific Reports 2025", "194 students in a Harvard physics course, AI tutor at home vs active-learning class", "Median learning gains more than double those of the in-class group, in less time (median 49 minutes with the tutor vs a 60-minute lesson).", "A carefully designed tutor can teach well"],

None of these is an apprenticeship study, and each has limits: 27 apprentices, one school, two physics lessons, one company. Together they point the same way. **What decides the result is not whether learners use AI, but what the AI is built to do.**

<picture><source media="(max-width: 640px)" srcset="/images/blog/ki-ueben-vs-pruefung-mobile.svg" /><img src="/images/blog/ki-ueben-vs-pruefung.svg" alt="Bar chart: in practice with AI, plus 48 percent with plain ChatGPT and plus 127 percent with the tutor version. In the exam without AI, minus 17 percent for the ChatGPT group and no significant difference for the tutor group" /></picture>

The Turkish study shows the mechanism. The most common message in the plain ChatGPT group was "What is the answer?", and many students copied what came back. When the researchers asked that question about the session problems, the model was right only about half the time, and the students did not notice that they had learned less. The tutor version was told to give hints, not answers, and was given the teachers' correct solutions to work from.

The Harvard tutor went further. Its authors designed it around seven principles of good teaching: active learning, managing cognitive load, a growth mindset, scaffolding, accurate information, timely feedback and self-pacing. One finding matters for anyone building such a tool: a system prompt alone could not provide enough structure, so the platform itself stepped students through each part. The authors also say plainly that an AI tutor should not replace in-person teaching.

## What a good AI tutor for apprentices does

Translated to the shop floor, the research gives a short specification:

- **It asks before it answers.** What have you tried? Which formula would you use? Then a hint, not the result.
- **It answers from your material.** The curriculum, your work instructions, your machine manuals, with the source attached, so a trainer can check it.
- **It says when it does not know.** In a trade where a wrong answer can mean a wrong wiring decision, an invented answer is a safety problem.
- **It steps through the task.** Structure comes from the tool, not from the apprentice's ability to write good prompts.
- **It tells the trainer where people are stuck.** Not to monitor anyone, but so the next workshop conversation starts in the right place.

## Three questions to test any AI tool

Before apprentices use a tool officially, give it three tasks yourself:

1. **A calculation from year one,** for example: "What current does a 2 kW load draw on a 230 V supply?" Does it give the result straight away, or ask which formula the apprentice would use?
2. **A question about your own material,** such as a machine or a work instruction from your site. Does it answer from your documents with a source, or guess from general knowledge?
3. **A question your material does not answer.** Does it say it does not know, or invent something plausible?

A tool that calculates instantly on question 1 and invents on question 3 can make apprentices faster without making them understand more, which is the pattern in the studies above. ## Your plan for the next cohort

1. **Map the classroom schedule** for year one and put your shop-floor tasks next to it.
2. **Pick one machine on site** as the thread through the first year's topics.
3. **Pair every calculation with a measurement** and every diagram with a prediction.
4. **Test every AI tool with the three questions** before you approve it.
5. **Agree rules for AI use** with your apprentices, rather than banning what they already use.
